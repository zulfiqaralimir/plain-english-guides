What is CISA, really?

CISA (Certified Information Systems Auditor) certifies that you can look at a company's computer systems and answer one question honestly: "Can we trust this?"

  • Checking whether data is accurate
  • Checking whether systems are secure
  • Checking whether IT is run responsibly
  • Checking whether the business could recover from a disaster

The exam is built around five domains — five different "trust questions" an auditor asks.

Auditor's jobCheck, don't fix — report facts with evidence
Core ideaConfidentiality, Integrity, Availability of information
MindsetRisk-based: spend attention where the damage would be worst
1

The Auditing Process

≈18% of exam
In one line: the rulebook for planning an audit, gathering proof, and reporting findings fairly — so two different auditors would reach the same conclusion.

Audit standards & ethics

  • ISACA sets standards auditors must follow — like independence and due care
  • Keeps audits consistent and trustworthy, no matter who performs them

Risk-based planning

  • You can't audit everything every year
  • List all the "auditable things" — the audit universe
  • Rank them by risk, then audit the riskiest areas first

Gathering evidence

  • Findings must be backed by evidence that is Sufficient, Reliable, Relevant, Useful — SRRU
  • Methods: interviews, watching a process happen, inspecting documents, re-doing a calculation yourself

Sampling

  • Checking every transaction is too slow — auditors test a sample
  • Statistical sampling uses math to pick objectively; judgmental sampling relies on the auditor's experience

Reporting & follow-up

  • A good report states the finding, the risk, and a practical fix
  • The auditor returns later to confirm the fix actually happened

Agile auditing & AI

  • Agile auditing works in short cycles alongside agile development teams instead of one long audit at the end
  • AI/ML tools are increasingly used to scan huge datasets for anomalies — but the auditor must still question how the algorithm reached its result before trusting it
Materiality — how big/important an issue is Independence — no conflict of interest CAATs — software that helps test data Audit charter — document giving auditors authority
Remember it as: a detective who plans the case (risk-based plan), collects proof (evidence), and writes an honest report (findings) — never fixes the crime scene themselves.
2

Governance & Management of IT

≈18% of exam
In one line: is IT being steered toward business goals, with clear ownership, sensible risk-taking, and someone accountable when things go wrong?

Governance frameworks

  • COBIT is ISACA's framework for governing and managing enterprise IT
  • Groups practices into goals like "align IT with the business" and "manage risk"
  • A checklist for running IT well — not a technical build standard

Strategy & structure

  • IT strategy should support the business strategy, not run off on its own
  • A RACI chart (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) prevents "everyone assumed someone else was doing it"
  • The Three Lines Model: operational management owns risk day to day (1st line), risk/compliance functions oversee it (2nd line), and internal audit independently checks both (3rd line)

Segregation of duties

  • The person who requests a payment shouldn't also approve and process it
  • Splitting duties prevents one person from making and hiding a mistake or fraud

Risk management

  • Identify what could go wrong; estimate likelihood and impact
  • Choose to avoid, reduce, transfer (e.g. insurance), or accept the risk
  • Guided by the organization's risk appetite

Performance & resourcing

  • KPIs/KRIs track whether IT delivers value and whether risk is creeping up
  • Also covers managing staff, vendors, contracts, and SLAs with outsourced providers
COBIT — ISACA's IT governance framework Risk appetite — how much risk is acceptable SoD — segregation of duties SLA — service-level agreement
Remember it as: the "management" chapter — who's in charge, who's watching risk, and whether IT is actually helping the business win.
3

Building & Buying Systems

≈12% of exam
In one line: when a company builds or buys a new system, was it planned, tested, and rolled out with proper controls — not rushed and hoped for the best?

Business case & project management

  • Before coding starts: a business case (why build this?) and a feasibility study
  • Projects run against a defined scope, schedule, budget, and quality target
  • Function point analysis estimates how big/costly a system will be by counting its inputs, outputs, and files — before a line of code is written
  • A Gantt chart maps tasks against a timeline so schedule slippage is easy to spot

Development approaches

  • Waterfall moves step-by-step: requirements → design → build → test → launch
  • Agile builds in short cycles (sprints) with frequent feedback
  • DevOps/DevSecOps automates build-test-release so code ships continuously — security gets baked into every step (DevSecOps) rather than checked only at the end
  • Neither is "correct" — the auditor checks the chosen approach was followed consistently

Testing

  • Unit testing — one small piece
  • Integration testing — pieces working together
  • UAT (User Acceptance Testing) — real users confirm it works before go-live

Change & configuration management

  • Every change should be requested, reviewed, approved, tested, and documented
  • Prevents anyone quietly slipping an unauthorized change into production

Application controls

  • Input controls — reject bad data before it's saved
  • Processing controls — calculations are correct
  • Output controls — reports match what was processed
SDLC — system development life cycle UAT — user acceptance testing Rollback plan — how to undo a failed release PIR — post-implementation review
Remember it as: "measure twice, cut once" — the whole domain is about disciplined process from idea to go-live.
4

Operations & Business Resilience

≈26% of exam
In one line: the biggest domain — does IT run smoothly day-to-day, and can the business keep going (or bounce back fast) when something breaks?

Day-to-day IT operations

  • Job scheduling, capacity planning, and handling problems
  • An incident is a single disruption fixed quickly
  • A problem is the underlying root cause behind repeated incidents

Data & infrastructure

  • Database management and network basics
  • Risk of end-user computing — e.g. a critical spreadsheet built by one employee with no backup or review
  • Shadow IT — apps or cloud services staff adopt without IT's knowledge, bypassing every control that was supposed to protect company data

Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

  • Before planning recovery, know what actually matters
  • Which systems, if down, would hurt the business fastest and most
  • How long the business can survive without each one

BCP & DRP

  • BCP keeps the whole business running during a disruption
  • DRP restores IT systems specifically
  • RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how fast must it be back up?
  • RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much data loss can we tolerate?

Alternate sites & backups

  • Hot site — ready to take over in minutes
  • Warm site — needs some setup
  • Cold site — empty shell needing days
  • Regular, tested backups make recovery actually possible
  • The 3-2-1 rule: keep 3 copies of data, on 2 different media types, with 1 copy offsite
RTO — time to recover RPO — acceptable data loss window BIA — business impact analysis Hot / warm / cold site — recovery-readiness levels
Remember it as: "keep the lights on, and know exactly how to turn them back on fast if they go out."
5

Protecting Information Assets

≈26% of exam
In one line: the security domain — keeping data and systems safe from people who shouldn't see, change, or destroy them.

The CIA triad

  • Confidentiality — only the right people see it
  • Integrity — data isn't altered without authorization
  • Availability — it's there when needed

Access control

  • Identification — who are you?
  • Authentication — can you prove it? (e.g. password + phone code = MFA)
  • Authorization — what are you allowed to do?
  • Give people only the access they need — least privilege

Modern identity concepts

  • Zero-Trust Architecture — "never trust, always verify": no user or device is trusted by default, even inside the network
  • Privileged Access Management (PAM) — extra controls around admin-level accounts, since they can do the most damage if compromised
  • Identity as a Service (IDaaS) — outsourcing identity/login management to a cloud provider instead of running it in-house

Network & endpoint security

  • Firewalls control what traffic gets in or out
  • IDS/IPS watch for attacks
  • Segmentation keeps a breach from spreading everywhere
  • Antivirus/EDR protects individual devices

Cryptography

  • Encryption scrambles data so only the right key unlocks it
  • Hashing creates a fingerprint to detect if data was changed
  • Digital signatures prove who sent something, unaltered

Monitoring, training & response

  • Logging / SIEM tools spot suspicious activity
  • Security-awareness training reduces human error (e.g. phishing clicks)
  • An incident response plan defines what to do the moment something goes wrong
CIA triad — confidentiality, integrity, availability MFA — multi-factor authentication Least privilege — minimum access needed SIEM — security event monitoring tool
Remember it as: the "lock, alarm, and camera" domain — prevent, detect, and respond.

Quick Glossary

Audit evidence
Proof behind every finding — must be sufficient, reliable, relevant, useful.
Control
Anything put in place to reduce risk — a policy, an approval step, a firewall, a lock.
Residual risk
The risk left over after controls are applied — never zero.
Due care / diligence
Doing what a reasonably careful professional would do in the same situation.
Segregation of duties
No single person controls a task start to finish, to prevent fraud or error.
RTO / RPO
How fast to recover, and how much data you can afford to lose, after a disruption.
Least privilege
Give access only to what a person needs to do their job — nothing more.
Root cause
The real underlying reason a problem keeps happening, not just its symptom.

How to Study This

  • Read domain by domain, not front-to-back — each one is a self-contained "chapter."
  • For every concept, ask: "what could go wrong without this, and how would an auditor catch it?" — that's the exam's real question format.
  • Learn the acronyms cold (RTO, RPO, SoD, MFA, CIA, RACI) — they appear constantly, in both directions.
  • Domains 4 and 5 carry over half the exam weight (52% combined) — study them hardest.
  • Practice with the CISA Review Questions manual after each domain to check whether you can apply the idea, not just recall it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which topics do most people find the most difficult?

Most candidates struggle most with Domain 4 (Operations & Business Resilience) and Domain 5 (Protection of Information Assets) — they're the most technical, cover the widest range of topics (networking, cryptography, BCP/DRP math like RTO/RPO), and together make up 52% of the exam. Many also find the sampling and evidence calculations in Domain 1 tricky at first, simply because they feel more like statistics than IT.

Do I need an IT or technical background to take CISA?

No — CISA is aimed at auditors, so a business or accounting background works fine. You do need to eventually explain technical controls in plain terms, which is exactly what this guide is for.

I'm a qualified Management Accountant (CMA) — which topics will be hardest, and what should I start learning first?

Good news first: your background already covers a lot of Domain 1 (Auditing Process) and Domain 2 (Governance) — internal controls, segregation of duties, risk management, and reporting findings will feel familiar since they're close to financial audit concepts.

What will be hardest: Domain 4 (Operations & Business Resilience) and Domain 5 (Protection of Information Assets) — these are the purely technical domains (networks, servers, encryption, access control) that an accounting background doesn't naturally cover. Domain 3 (Building Systems) will also take extra effort since it uses software-development vocabulary you likely haven't used day to day.

Where to start building concepts:

  • Basic IT infrastructure: what a server, database, and network actually are
  • Networking fundamentals: firewalls, IP addresses, what a "protocol" means
  • Core security ideas: the CIA triad, encryption, access control, MFA
  • SDLC vocabulary: waterfall vs. agile, and the different types of testing
  • BCP/DR mechanics: RTO, RPO, backup types, hot/warm/cold sites
How much work experience do I need?

ISACA requires 5 years of relevant IS audit, control, or security work experience to be certified. You can take the exam first — the experience requirement can be completed within 5 years after passing.

How is the exam scored?

Scores run from 200 to 800, and you need 450 to pass. It's a scaled score, not a raw percentage, so the exact number of correct answers needed varies slightly by exam form.

Which domain should I study first?

Start with Domain 1 — it teaches the audit vocabulary and mindset (evidence, risk-based planning, reporting) that the other four domains all assume you already know.

Do I need to renew the certification after passing?

Yes — CISA requires ongoing Continuing Professional Education (CPE) hours reported annually, plus an annual maintenance fee, to keep the certification active.

I want to build an IT-audit software that uses AI to automate the process in real time — what should I learn and do?

This is essentially building a Continuous Control Monitoring (CCM) platform with AI layered on top. Here's a practical path:

1. Learn the audit domain first, not the code first. You can't automate a control test you don't understand. Study CISA Domain 1 closely — especially CAATs (Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques), continuous auditing/monitoring, and the manual's own section on AI/ML in IS audit (interpreting AI results and their risk). This tells you what a "good" automated test looks like and where AI can go wrong.

2. Learn the technical stack:

  • Python + SQL — the backbone for pulling and analyzing data
  • APIs/integrations — to pull real-time data from ERPs, cloud logs (AWS CloudTrail, Azure AD), ticketing systems, and SIEM tools
  • Rule engines — for deterministic checks (e.g., segregation-of-duty conflicts, terminated employee still has access)
  • ML for anomaly detection — to catch patterns a fixed rule would miss (unusual transaction timing, amount, or user behavior)
  • Dashboards/alerting — so exceptions surface the moment they happen, not in a report weeks later

3. Start narrow, not broad. Pick one control (e.g., user access recertification, or duplicate vendor payments) and automate that single test end-to-end before expanding. A working narrow tool beats a half-built platform.

4. Build in auditability of the AI itself. Every flagged exception needs an explainable "why," a human sign-off step, and its own audit trail — regulators and external auditors will ask "how did the AI decide this?" and "no answer" is not acceptable. This is the exact gap the CISA manual flags under AI/ML audit risk.

Is CISA the same as CISSP? How are they different?

No — they're different certifications for different jobs, even though both come up in security careers:

Role: CISA is for auditors — checking whether systems, controls, and processes can be trusted. CISSP is for security practitioners — designing, building, and running the security program itself.

Focus: CISA leans on audit process, evidence, governance, and risk (5 domains). CISSP leans on deep technical security across its own 8 domains, defined by (ISC)²:

  • Security & Risk Management — overlaps with CISA Domain 2, Governance
  • Asset Security — touches CISA Domain 5, Protecting Assets
  • Security Architecture & Engineering — touches CISA Domain 5
  • Communication & Network Security — overlaps with CISA Domain 5
  • Identity & Access Management (IAM) — overlaps with CISA Domain 5
  • Security Assessment & Testing — overlaps with CISA Domain 1, Auditing Process
  • Security Operations — overlaps with CISA Domain 4, Operations & Resilience
  • Software Development Security — overlaps with CISA Domain 3, Building Systems

In short: IAM, network security, and cryptography (CISSP's Communication & Network Security + Identity & Access Management + parts of Architecture & Engineering) map closely onto CISA Domain 5, while Security Assessment & Testing maps onto CISA Domain 1. The rest is where the two certifications genuinely diverge — CISSP goes deeper into building and engineering; CISA goes deeper into auditing and governance.

Issuer: CISA is issued by ISACA; CISSP is issued by (ISC)².

Experience needed: CISA needs 5 years of IS audit/control/security experience. CISSP needs 5 years of experience in at least 2 of its 8 domains (or 4 years with a relevant degree).

Typical roles after: CISA → IT auditor, compliance manager, risk analyst. CISSP → security engineer, security architect, CISO track. Some professionals hold both — CISA to speak the auditor's language, CISSP to speak the builder's language.

Which is harder? Most people rate CISSP as the tougher exam — it covers deep technical ground across 8 broad domains and is known for tricky "choose the best answer" questions. CISA is generally seen as more approachable if you think in terms of process and risk rather than deep technical detail — but it's still a serious, respected exam, not an easy one.
