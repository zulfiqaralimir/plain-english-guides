Governance vs. Management: The CISA Distinction That Trips Everyone Up

In everyday speech, "who's governing this" and "who's managing this" sound like the same question. On the exam — and in a real audit finding — they're answered by two different people.

Domain 1 of the CISA exam leans on this distinction constantly, and it's easy to blur because both words describe "people in charge of stuff." The cleanest way to keep them apart is to ask a different question of each: governance asks are we doing the right things? Management asks are we doing things right?

Governance sets direction and watches from above

Governance is the board and senior leadership's job. It's about setting strategy, approving policy, allocating resources, and holding management accountable for outcomes. Governance doesn't execute — it evaluates, directs, and monitors. A board doesn't configure a firewall; it approves the security policy that says a firewall is required and then asks for evidence that policy is being followed.

Management executes within the direction it's given

Management — IT management, security management — plans, builds, runs, and monitors the actual operations. They translate a governance-level policy ("all remote access requires MFA") into a working control (an actual MFA rollout, a help desk process, a monitoring dashboard). Management reports upward; governance evaluates what's reported and adjusts direction accordingly.

Why the exam cares so much

A recurring question pattern describes a scenario — say, a security incident caused by a missing patching process — and asks whether it represents a governance failure or a management failure. The trick is that it's often both, at different levels: management failed to execute patching consistently, and governance failed by not requiring reporting that would have surfaced the gap sooner. CISA wants you to recognize that a control breakdown at the operational level frequently traces back to an oversight gap at the governance level — that's the whole reason governance exists, to catch what management alone would miss.

A concrete example

Imagine an organization has no formal data classification policy, and as a result, sensitive customer data ends up on a shared drive with broad access. Is that a management problem or a governance problem?

  • If a classification policy exists but employees weren't trained or the drive wasn't configured to enforce it — that's a management execution gap.
  • If no policy exists at all, and no one at the leadership level required one — that's a governance gap. Management can't manage a rule that governance never set.
Remember it as

Governance writes the rulebook and checks the scoreboard. Management plays the game. If there's no rulebook, don't blame the players.

Once you have this frame, a lot of Domain 1 questions stop being about memorizing definitions and start being about correctly locating where in the org chart a given failure actually originated.
