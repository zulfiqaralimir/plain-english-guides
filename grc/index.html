What is GRC — and what is CGRC, really?

GRC (Governance, Risk, and Compliance) is the discipline of running security as a managed program rather than a pile of individual controls: governance sets who decides what, risk management decides how much danger is acceptable, and compliance proves the organization is actually doing what it promised regulators, customers, and auditors.

CGRC (Certified in Governance, Risk and Compliance) — formerly called CAP, Certified Authorization Professional — is (ISC)²'s certification for the people who actually run that program day to day, using the Risk Management Framework (RMF): the formal, repeatable process the U.S. federal government (and many private organizations) use to authorize an information system for use.

  • Answering "is this system allowed to operate, and under what conditions?"
  • Turning abstract risk appetite into a concrete set of approved controls
  • Producing the paper trail — SSP, SAR, POA&M — that lets a decision-maker actually decide
  • Keeping that authorization honest after go-live, not just on day one

The exam is built around seven domains that map directly onto the seven steps of the RMF life cycle.

1

Information Security Risk Management Program

≈15% of exam
In one line: the foundation domain — understanding the organization's risk management structure, the legal/regulatory backdrop, and who is accountable for what before any system-level work starts.

Organization-wide risk management

  • Risk is managed at three tiers: organization, mission/business process, and information system — decisions at one tier constrain the others
  • A documented risk management strategy sets the organization's risk appetite and tolerance up front

Legal, regulatory & framework context

  • Drivers include FISMA, the Privacy Act, and sector-specific rules; private-sector equivalents lean on ISO/IEC 27001 or industry frameworks
  • CGRC applies RMF concepts that generalize beyond U.S. federal systems to any organization running a formal ATO-style process

Key roles

  • Authorizing Official (AO) — the senior official who accepts risk and grants the authorization
  • System Owner, ISSO (Information System Security Officer), and Common Control Provider each own a different slice of the process

Integrating privacy

  • Privacy risk is assessed alongside security risk, not bolted on afterward — a Senior Agency Official for Privacy role mirrors the AO for privacy decisions

Program-level documentation

  • Policies, standards, and the risk management strategy give every later domain a consistent baseline to build from
RMF — Risk Management Framework AO — Authorizing Official Risk tolerance — how much risk is acceptable ISSO — Information System Security Officer
Remember it as: the org chart and rulebook domain — who is allowed to accept risk, and under which laws and policies.
2

Scope of the Information System

≈14% of exam
In one line: deciding exactly what the "system" is — its boundary, its data, and how much damage its loss would cause — before you can protect it correctly.

Categorization

  • FIPS 199 categorizes a system as Low, Moderate, or High impact for each of confidentiality, integrity, and availability
  • The highest of those three ratings drives the overall system categorization — a "high-water mark" rule

System boundary

  • The authorization boundary defines exactly which components, interfaces, and data flows are "inside" the system being authorized
  • Drawing it too loosely hides risk; drawing it too tightly creates gaps at the edges

Information types & stakeholders

  • Every data type processed (PII, financial, operational) gets its own impact assessment
  • Stakeholders — owners, users, mission partners — are identified early since they shape requirements

System description & architecture

  • A written system description captures purpose, architecture, and interconnections — this becomes the backbone of the System Security Plan (SSP) later

Registering the system

  • The system is entered into the organization's system inventory so it's tracked for oversight and resourcing
FIPS 199 — impact categorization standard Authorization boundary — what's "in scope" High-water mark — highest impact rating wins SSP — System Security Plan
Remember it as: drawing the fence — you can't protect a system you haven't defined the edges of.
3

Selection and Approval of Security and Privacy Controls

≈19% of exam
In one line: the heaviest domain — picking the right catalog of controls for the system's risk level, then tailoring and documenting them so everyone agrees what "secure" means for this system.

Control baselines

  • NIST SP 800-53 catalogs hundreds of controls, grouped into Low, Moderate, and High baselines that match the FIPS 199 categorization from Domain 2
  • A baseline is a starting point, not a final answer

Tailoring

  • Scoping guidance, compensating controls, and organization-defined parameters adjust the baseline to fit the real environment
  • Every tailoring decision needs a documented rationale — an assessor will ask why later

Common vs. system-specific controls

  • Common controls are inherited from elsewhere (e.g. a shared data center's physical security) and managed by a Common Control Provider
  • System-specific controls are implemented and owned entirely within this system's boundary

Documenting the plan

  • Selected and tailored controls, plus how each will be implemented, get written into the System Security Plan (SSP)
  • Privacy controls follow the same selection logic via a parallel privacy control baseline

Review & approval

  • The AO or a designated representative reviews and approves the control selection before implementation begins — catching gaps early is far cheaper than catching them at assessment
SP 800-53 — NIST control catalog Baseline — starting set of controls by impact level Tailoring — adjusting controls to fit context Common control — inherited, shared control
Remember it as: the shopping-list domain — choosing exactly which controls this specific system needs, and writing down why.
4

Implementation of Security and Privacy Controls

≈15% of exam
In one line: actually building the controls the SSP promised — and documenting exactly how each one was implemented, so it can be verified later.

Turning the plan into practice

  • Technical, operational, and management controls are configured, deployed, or put into procedure exactly as described in the SSP
  • Gaps between "planned" and "actually built" have to be reconciled, not ignored

Documenting implementation details

  • For each control, the SSP is updated with the specific implementation detail — not just "encryption is used" but which algorithm, key length, and configuration
  • Vague documentation is one of the most common findings during later assessment

Coordinating across teams

  • Implementation usually spans system engineers, developers, and facilities — the ISSO coordinates so nothing falls through the cracks

Privacy controls in practice

  • Privacy controls (data minimization, consent mechanisms, retention limits) are implemented alongside security controls, not as a separate afterthought project
Control implementation statement — the "how," in detail Technical control — enforced by technology Operational control — enforced by people/process Management control — enforced by oversight/policy
Remember it as: "build it and write down exactly what you built" — an unwritten control is indistinguishable from a missing one during assessment.
5

Assessment/Audit of Security and Privacy Controls

≈13% of exam
In one line: an independent check on whether the implemented controls actually work as documented — trust, but verify, with a paper trail.

Assessment planning

  • A Security Assessment Plan (SAP) defines scope, methods (examine, interview, test), and the assessment team
  • The assessor should be independent enough that they aren't grading their own homework

Conducting the assessment

  • A Security Control Assessor (SCA) executes the SAP, gathering evidence for every control claimed in the SSP
  • Findings are rated by how much residual risk they represent, not just pass/fail

Reporting results

  • Results go into a Security Assessment Report (SAR) — what was tested, what passed, what didn't, and why it matters

Remediation & POA&M

  • Findings that aren't fixed immediately go into a Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M) — what will be fixed, by whom, and by when
  • The POA&M becomes one of the three documents the AO reviews to authorize the system in Domain 6
SAP — Security Assessment Plan SCA — Security Control Assessor SAR — Security Assessment Report POA&M — Plan of Action & Milestones
Remember it as: the "prove it" domain — a documented control claim only counts once someone independent has tested it.
6

Authorization/Approval of Information System

≈13% of exam
In one line: the moment of decision — a senior official reviews the full risk picture and formally accepts (or rejects) the risk of operating the system.

The authorization package

  • The AO's decision rests on three documents: the SSP (what was promised), the SAR (what was verified), and the POA&M (what's still outstanding)

Risk determination

  • The AO weighs residual risk against the organization's risk tolerance from Domain 1 — a system can have known gaps and still be authorized if the risk is acceptable and tracked

Authorization decisions

  • ATO (Authority to Operate) — full approval, typically time-limited
  • Interim ATO (IATO) — temporary, conditional approval while gaps are closed
  • Denial of Authorization — the system may not operate until risk is addressed

Communicating the decision

  • The authorization decision, its conditions, and its expiration are documented and communicated to all stakeholders — not just filed away
ATO — Authority to Operate Risk acceptance — a formal, documented decision Authorization package — SSP + SAR + POA&M Residual risk — what's left after controls are applied
Remember it as: the "sign here" domain — someone accountable puts their name on the risk, in writing.
7

Continuous Monitoring

≈11% of exam
In one line: an authorization is a snapshot, not a guarantee — this domain keeps that snapshot honest for as long as the system stays in operation.

Ongoing assessment

  • Controls are re-checked on a rolling schedule instead of waiting for the next full reauthorization
  • An ISCM (Information Security Continuous Monitoring) strategy defines what gets checked, how often, and by whom

Security status reporting

  • Regular status reports keep the AO informed between formal reauthorizations, so risk decisions stay current rather than stale

Ongoing POA&M management

  • Outstanding items are tracked to closure, and new findings from monitoring or incidents feed straight back into the POA&M

Change management

  • Significant changes to the system can trigger a reassessment of affected controls before the change goes live — authorization doesn't automatically survive a major redesign

Decommissioning

  • When a system is retired, its authorization is formally closed out and its data is disposed of or migrated per policy — the life cycle has an end, not just a start
ISCM — Information Security Continuous Monitoring Ongoing authorization — risk-based, not calendar-based Reauthorization — renewed formal risk decision Decommissioning — formal system retirement
Remember it as: the "don't set and forget" domain — the authorization is only as good as the monitoring that keeps it true.

Quick Glossary

RMF
The Risk Management Framework — the seven-step life cycle this entire certification is built around.
AO
The Authorizing Official — the person who formally accepts risk and grants the ATO.
SSP
System Security Plan — documents the system, its boundary, and every selected control's implementation.
SAR
Security Assessment Report — the independent assessor's findings on whether controls actually work.
POA&M
Plan of Action & Milestones — the tracked to-do list of unresolved findings, owners, and dates.
ATO
Authority to Operate — the AO's formal, often time-limited, approval for the system to run.
Residual risk
The risk that remains after controls are applied — never zero, and it's what the AO ultimately accepts.
Continuous monitoring
Ongoing, risk-based verification that controls still work — keeps the authorization honest after day one.

How to Study This

  • Learn the seven domains in order — they aren't arbitrary categories, they're the seven sequential steps of the RMF life cycle, so the order itself is testable.
  • Master the document trail: SSP → SAR → POA&M → authorization package. A huge share of exam questions boil down to "which document does this belong in?"
  • Domain 3 (Selecting Controls) carries the most weight at ≈19% — know SP 800-53 baselines and tailoring cold.
  • Think like the AO, not the engineer — CGRC questions reward risk-based judgment calls over purely technical fixes.
  • Keep governance vocabulary straight from the other two disciplines: GRC decides "should we accept this risk," security engineering decides "how do we build the control," and auditing decides "can we prove it happened."

Frequently Asked Questions

Is CGRC the same thing as CAP?

Yes — CGRC (Certified in Governance, Risk and Compliance) is the current name for what was previously called CAP (Certified Authorization Professional). (ISC)² renamed it to better reflect that the skill set applies broadly to GRC roles, not only to formal U.S. federal authorization work.

Which domain do most candidates find hardest?

Domain 3 (Selection and Approval of Controls) trips up the most people — it carries the heaviest exam weight and requires genuine familiarity with the NIST SP 800-53 control catalog and tailoring logic, not just memorized definitions.

Do I need to work for the U.S. federal government to use this?

No — CGRC is built around the federal RMF, but the underlying pattern (categorize → select controls → implement → assess → authorize → monitor) is a general-purpose GRC methodology used by private organizations, contractors, and other governments that run formal risk-acceptance processes.

How much work experience do I need?

(ISC)² requires 2 years of cumulative, paid work experience in one or more of the seven CGRC domains. If you pass the exam without that experience yet, you become an Associate of (ISC)² and have time to complete it.

Which domain should I study first?

Start with Domain 1 (Risk Management Program) — it introduces the roles (AO, ISSO, Common Control Provider) and vocabulary that every later domain assumes you already know.

How does GRC/CGRC relate to CISA and CISSP?

All three sit in the same security-career family but ask a different core question:

CGRC (this guide) asks "should this specific system be allowed to operate, and under what accepted risk?" — it's the formal, document-driven authorization process (RMF).

CISA asks "can we trust this system and its controls?" — an auditor's independent, evidence-based check across five domains.

CISSP asks "how do we actually design and build a system that's trustworthy?" — a practitioner's hands-on, technical build-and-operate view across eight domains.

Overlap: CGRC's Domain 5 (Assessment) closely mirrors CISA's audit mindset and CISSP's Security Assessment & Testing domain; CGRC's Domain 1 (Risk Management Program) overlaps with CISSP's Security & Risk Management and CISA's Governance domain. CGRC is narrower and more process-specific than either — it's less "know security broadly" and more "run the RMF life cycle correctly, end to end."

Issuer: CGRC and CISSP are both issued by (ISC)²; CISA is issued by ISACA. Many GRC-track professionals hold CGRC alongside CISA or CISSP since the three reinforce rather than duplicate each other.

Do I need to renew the certification after passing?

Yes — like other (ISC)² credentials, CGRC requires ongoing Continuing Professional Education (CPE) credits on a multi-year cycle, plus an annual maintenance fee, to stay active.
