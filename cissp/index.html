What is CISSP, really?

CISSP (Certified Information Systems Security Professional) certifies that you can design, build, and run a security program end to end — not just audit one. Where an auditor asks "can we trust this?", a CISSP holder answers a different question: "how do we actually make this trustworthy?"

  • Designing security that fits the business's risk appetite
  • Protecting data and systems across their entire lifecycle
  • Building networks, architectures, and code that are secure by design
  • Running the people, processes, and tools that detect and respond when something goes wrong

The exam is built around eight domains — eight different slices of the security practitioner's job.

1

Security & Risk Management

≈15% of exam
In one line: the foundation domain — policy, law, ethics, and risk decisions that every other domain is built on top of.

CIA triad & governance

  • Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — the three things every control ultimately protects
  • Security governance ties the program to business objectives, with policies, standards, and procedures cascading down from senior management

Legal, regulatory & compliance

  • Laws differ by country — GDPR, sector rules, intellectual property, contract law
  • Understanding civil vs. criminal vs. regulatory liability shapes how incidents are handled

Risk management

  • Identify assets and threats, assess likelihood and impact, then avoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept the risk
  • Guided by the organization's risk appetite and risk tolerance

Business continuity basics

  • BCP planning starts here with a Business Impact Analysis (BIA)
  • Domain 7 later covers actually executing the plan during a disruption

Personnel security & training

  • Background checks, onboarding/offboarding, and security-awareness training reduce human-factor risk
  • Employees are often the weakest link — training is a control, not an afterthought

Threat modeling & supply chain

  • Threat modeling (e.g. STRIDE) identifies how a system could be attacked before it's built
  • Supply-chain risk management — vetting vendors and third-party software, since a breach anywhere in the chain becomes your breach
CIA triad — confidentiality, integrity, availability Risk appetite — how much risk is acceptable Due diligence — reasonable care taken before acting BIA — business impact analysis
Remember it as: the constitution of the whole CBK — the policy and risk decisions that every other domain has to obey.
2

Asset Security

≈10% of exam
In one line: knowing what data and assets you own, labeling them correctly, and protecting each one appropriately across its entire life.

Classification & ownership

  • Data gets labeled — e.g. Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted — so everyone knows how carefully to handle it
  • Every asset needs a named owner accountable for its protection

Data roles

  • Owner decides classification and access rules
  • Custodian handles day-to-day technical protection (backups, encryption)
  • Steward ensures data quality and proper use

Data lifecycle

  • Create → store → use → share → archive → destroy
  • Protection requirements can change at each stage — data in transit needs different controls than data at rest

Protection controls

  • Encryption at rest and in transit, access controls, and backups
  • DLP (Data Loss Prevention) tools stop sensitive data leaving the organization through email, USB, or uploads

Retention & disposal

  • Keep data only as long as legally/operationally required — a retention policy
  • Secure disposal (degaussing, shredding, cryptographic erasure) prevents data remanence — recoverable traces left after deletion
Data classification — sensitivity labeling Data remanence — residual data after "deletion" DLP — data loss prevention Data custodian — handles technical protection
Remember it as: label it, own it, protect it, and dispose of it properly when it's no longer needed.
3

Security Architecture & Engineering

≈13% of exam
In one line: designing systems, cryptography, and physical spaces so they're secure by construction — not patched after the fact.

Security models

  • Bell-LaPadula — protects confidentiality ("no read up, no write down")
  • Biba — protects integrity (the mirror-image opposite)
  • These formal models underpin how access-control systems are designed

Secure design principles

  • Defense in depth — layer controls so one failure isn't fatal
  • Least privilege, fail secure, and Zero Trust — never trust by default, always verify

Cryptography fundamentals

  • Symmetric encryption (one shared key, fast) vs. asymmetric (public/private key pair)
  • Hashing fingerprints data; PKI issues and manages the certificates that make trust scalable

System & hardware security

  • TPM chips anchor trust in hardware; secure boot stops tampered firmware from loading
  • Virtualization and cloud introduce shared-tenancy risks that need their own controls

Physical & environmental security

  • Site design, fencing, badges, and environmental controls (fire suppression, HVAC, power) protect the physical layer
  • A perfect digital design still fails if someone can walk into the server room
Bell-LaPadula — confidentiality model PKI — public key infrastructure TPM — trusted platform module Defense in depth — layered controls
Remember it as: the engineering domain — building security into the blueprint, not bolting it on afterward.
4

Communication & Network Security

≈13% of exam
In one line: how data moves safely between systems — and how to stop it being intercepted, altered, or blocked along the way.

Network architecture

  • The OSI (7-layer) and TCP/IP models frame where each protocol and attack operates
  • Segmentation and VLANs stop a breach in one zone from spreading everywhere

Secure network components

  • Firewalls filter traffic; IDS/IPS detect and block attacks in progress
  • Routers, switches, and proxies each need their own hardening baseline

Secure communication channels

  • VPNs tunnel traffic securely across untrusted networks
  • TLS secures web traffic in transit; email and voice need their own protections too

Wireless security

  • WPA3 and strong authentication protect Wi-Fi from eavesdropping and rogue access points
  • Wireless removes the physical-cable barrier, so authentication matters even more

Cloud & converged communications

  • SD-WAN and SDN centralize network control in software rather than per-device configuration
  • VoIP and unified communications bring their own eavesdropping and toll-fraud risks
OSI model — 7-layer networking reference VPN — virtual private network TLS — transport layer security Segmentation — isolating network zones
Remember it as: the plumbing domain — making sure data travels through pipes that can't be tapped, poisoned, or shut off.
5

Identity & Access Management (IAM)

≈13% of exam
In one line: proving who someone is, deciding what they're allowed to touch, and making sure access disappears the moment it shouldn't exist.

IAAA

  • Identification — who are you claiming to be?
  • Authentication — can you prove it? (password + phone code = MFA)
  • Authorization — what are you allowed to do?
  • Accountability — logging ties actions back to an identity

Access control models

  • DAC — owner decides access; MAC — a central policy decides, users can't override it
  • RBAC — access tied to job role; ABAC — access decided by attributes (department, time, location) at request time

Federated identity & SSO

  • Single Sign-On lets one login work across many systems
  • Federation (SAML, OAuth, OIDC) extends trust across organizational boundaries without sharing passwords

Provisioning lifecycle

  • Access should be granted on joining, adjusted on role change, and revoked on departure — every time, without exception
  • Periodic access recertification catches accounts that should've been removed already

Privileged access & zero trust

  • PAM (Privileged Access Management) — extra controls around admin accounts, since they cause the most damage if compromised
  • Zero Trust — verify every request continuously, regardless of network location
MFA — multi-factor authentication SSO — single sign-on RBAC — role-based access control Least privilege — minimum access needed
Remember it as: prove it, permit it, and take it away the second it's no longer needed.
6

Security Assessment & Testing

≈12% of exam
In one line: trust, but verify — constantly checking that the controls you built are actually working the way you think they are.

Assessment & test strategies

  • Vulnerability scans find known weaknesses automatically; penetration tests actively try to exploit them
  • Tests can be black-box (no inside knowledge), white-box (full knowledge), or gray-box (partial)

Security control testing

  • Code review, interface testing, and security-control assessments confirm a control does what it claims
  • Results feed back into risk decisions — a failing control changes the risk picture

Logs & process data

  • Log review, synthetic transactions, and KPI/KRI tracking give ongoing visibility, not just point-in-time snapshots

Audits

  • Internal audits are run by the organization; external/third-party audits (e.g. SOC 2) are run independently for outside assurance

Reporting & analysis

  • Findings must translate into a report leadership can act on: what's broken, how risky is it, what fixes it
Pen test — simulated attack Vulnerability scan — automated weakness check SOC 2 — third-party assurance audit Audit trail — logged record of activity
Remember it as: the "prove it's actually working" domain — controls only count if they're tested.
7

Security Operations

≈13% of exam
In one line: the day-to-day defense job — watching, responding, and recovering when something actually goes wrong.

Incident management

  • Standard lifecycle: detect → respond → mitigate → report → recover → lessons learned
  • A documented incident response plan keeps the team fast and consistent under pressure

Digital forensics

  • Evidence must preserve a chain of custody — an unbroken, documented trail — or it won't hold up in court
  • Forensic imaging captures evidence without altering the original

Detective & preventive measures

  • SIEM tools correlate logs across the environment to spot attacks in real time
  • Preventive controls (firewalls, patching) reduce how often incidents happen at all

BCP/DR execution

  • Domain 1 plans it; this domain runs it — activating recovery sites and restoring operations
  • RTO (how fast to recover) and RPO (how much data loss is tolerable) drive the recovery strategy

Change & physical operations

  • Formal change management stops unreviewed changes from becoming the next incident
  • Guards, monitoring, and access logs round out physical security operations
SIEM — security event monitoring Chain of custody — evidence integrity trail RTO / RPO — recovery time / data-loss targets IR plan — incident response plan
Remember it as: the "keep the lights on and put out fires" domain — the most hands-on, operational part of the CBK.
8

Software Development Security

≈11% of exam
In one line: baking security into the code itself throughout the build process, instead of trying to patch it in after release.

Secure SDLC

  • Security touchpoints at every phase: requirements, design, build, test, release, maintain
  • DevSecOps pushes security checks into the CI/CD pipeline itself, not a gate at the end

Development methodologies

  • Waterfall — sequential, plan-heavy; Agile — short iterative sprints with fast feedback
  • Security requirements need to be built into whichever methodology the team uses, not bolted on separately

Common vulnerabilities

  • The OWASP Top 10 catalogs the most frequent web-app risks — injection, broken access control, misconfiguration
  • Classic bugs like buffer overflows and race conditions still matter at the systems level

Secure coding & review

  • SAST (static analysis) scans source code; DAST (dynamic analysis) tests the running application
  • Peer code review catches what tools and a single developer both miss

Software supply chain

  • Open-source and third-party libraries can carry vulnerabilities straight into your product
  • A Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) tracks exactly what components ship inside an application
SDLC — software development life cycle OWASP Top 10 — common web-app risks SAST / DAST — static / dynamic code testing SBOM — software bill of materials
Remember it as: "measure twice, cut once" for code — catch the flaw in a code review, not in a breach report.

Quick Glossary

CIA triad
Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — the three properties every control protects.
Control
Anything put in place to reduce risk — a policy, an approval step, a firewall, a lock.
Residual risk
The risk left over after controls are applied — never zero.
Least privilege
Give access only to what a person needs to do their job — nothing more.
Defense in depth
Layer multiple controls so a single failure doesn't cause a full breach.
Zero Trust
Never trust by default — verify every user and device on every request.
RTO / RPO
How fast to recover, and how much data you can afford to lose, after a disruption.
Chain of custody
An unbroken, documented trail proving evidence wasn't tampered with.

How to Study This

  • Read domain by domain, not front-to-back — each one is a self-contained "chapter."
  • For every concept, ask: "how would I actually build or configure this?" — CISSP rewards practical, best-practice thinking, not memorized trivia.
  • Learn the acronyms cold (CIA, MFA, RBAC, RTO, RPO, SIEM, SDLC) — they appear constantly, in both directions.
  • CISSP is famous for "choose the best answer" questions where several options are technically correct — always pick the one that best fits the manager's perspective, not the most technically clever fix.
  • Domains 1, 3, 4, 5, and 7 each carry double-digit weight — study them hardest, but don't skip the smaller domains since every domain has a minimum passing threshold.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which domains do most people find the most difficult?

Most candidates struggle most with Domain 3 (Security Architecture & Engineering) because of its cryptography math and formal security models, and Domain 4 (Communication & Network Security) because of its dense networking detail. Domain 8 (Software Development Security) is also tough for candidates without a coding background.

Do I need a technical/coding background to take CISSP?

No — CISSP is intentionally broad and management-leaning rather than purely technical. A background in IT operations, risk, or audit works fine, though you'll need to build baseline familiarity with networking, cryptography, and secure coding concepts covered in Domains 3, 4, and 8.

How much work experience do I need?

(ISC)² requires 5 years of cumulative, paid work experience in at least 2 of the 8 domains (4 years if you hold a relevant four-year degree or an approved credential). If you pass the exam without enough experience yet, you become an Associate of (ISC)² and have up to 6 years to complete it.

How is the exam scored?

Scores run from 0 to 1000, and you need 700 to pass. The English exam uses Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) — each question's difficulty adjusts based on your previous answers, and the exam ends once the system is statistically confident in a pass/fail result (between 100 and 150 questions, in up to 3 hours).

Which domain should I study first?

Start with Domain 1 (Security & Risk Management) — it establishes the governance, risk, and CIA-triad vocabulary that every other domain assumes you already know.

Do I need to renew the certification after passing?

Yes — CISSP requires 120 Continuing Professional Education (CPE) credits every 3 years, plus an annual maintenance fee, to keep the certification active.

Is CISSP the same as CISA? How are they different?

No — they're different certifications for different jobs, even though both come up in security careers:

Role: CISSP is for security practitioners — designing, building, and running the security program itself. CISA is for auditors — checking whether systems, controls, and processes can be trusted.

Focus: CISSP leans on deep technical security across its own 8 domains. CISA leans on audit process, evidence, governance, and risk across its 5 domains:

  • Security & Risk Management (this guide) — overlaps with CISA Domain 2, Governance
  • Asset Security — touches CISA Domain 5, Protecting Assets
  • Security Architecture & Engineering — touches CISA Domain 5
  • Communication & Network Security — overlaps with CISA Domain 5
  • Identity & Access Management — overlaps with CISA Domain 5
  • Security Assessment & Testing — overlaps with CISA Domain 1, Auditing Process
  • Security Operations — overlaps with CISA Domain 4, Operations & Resilience
  • Software Development Security — overlaps with CISA Domain 3, Building Systems

Issuer: CISSP is issued by (ISC)²; CISA is issued by ISACA.

Typical roles after: CISSP → security engineer, security architect, CISO track. CISA → IT auditor, compliance manager, risk analyst. Some professionals hold both — CISSP to speak the builder's language, CISA to speak the auditor's language.

Which is harder? Most people rate CISSP as the tougher exam — it covers deep technical ground across 8 broad domains and is known for tricky "choose the best answer" questions. CISA is generally seen as more approachable if you think in terms of process and risk rather than deep technical detail.
